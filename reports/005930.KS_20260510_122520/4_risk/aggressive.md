I don’t dismiss that, but I strongly disagree with using it as a reason to avoid buying. In powerful institutional accumulation phases, overbought conditions often confirm strength rather than warn of immediate reversal. That is a global semiconductor leader trading at a valuation that still leaves room for significant rerating if earnings estimates move higher.

Operating profit was up roughly 65% quarter-on-quarter from Q3. That is exactly the kind of inflection investors should buy early in the cycle, not after every analyst has already raised numbers and the valuation gap has closed.

The conservative view points to CAPEX as a risk. I see it differently. Samsung’s heavy CAPEX is not a weakness in this environment; it is a competitive weapon. AI memory, HBM, advanced DRAM, NAND, and packaging require scale. Smaller or weaker competitors cannot match Samsung’s balance sheet. With 125.85 trillion won in cash and short-term investments and only 25.24 trillion won of debt, Samsung can invest through the cycle while others are forced to ration capital. That is how market leaders widen the gap.

The neutral and conservative sides also cite inventory and receivables growth. Again, that is overly defensive. Rising inventory in a falling-price cycle is dangerous. Rising inventory into a tightening memory market can be valuable. If DRAM and NAND prices are rising faster than expected, inventory becomes operating leverage. The Bernstein commentary that Q2 DRAM/NAND contract prices may rise more sharply than expected is exactly the backdrop where inventory concerns should be treated differently.

On customer cost pressure, the HOLD camp is also interpreting the evidence too cautiously. Sony and Nintendo complaining about memory price increases is not bearish for Samsung. It is confirmation of supplier pricing power. If customers are publicly acknowledging higher memory costs, that means the price increases are real, not theoretical. Yes, extreme price increases can eventually create demand elasticity, but in the current AI infrastructure cycle, high-performance memory is a bottleneck input. Data centers do not cancel strategic AI expansion because DRAM costs more. They compete to secure supply.

The labor negotiation risk is real, but it is being over-weighted. The report itself notes that after Reuters covered the wage dispute and possible mediation process, Samsung’s shares reportedly rose in after-hours trading by 2.6%. That is the market telling you this is a short-term volatility event, not a thesis-breaking problem. If the stock rises despite labor-risk headlines, then investors are clearly prioritizing the memory upcycle. Conservative analysts are treating this as a reason to wait; the market is treating it as noise unless it becomes an actual prolonged production disruption.

HBM competitiveness is another concern where I think caution misses the upside. Yes, SK Hynix has had a stronger HBM narrative. But that is precisely why Samsung has optionality. If Samsung merely closes the perception gap through customer qualification, yield improvement, or stronger HBM supply announcements, the stock can rerate sharply. The market already knows Samsung has lagged in HBM perception. What it may not fully price is the upside from regaining credibility. That is a classic aggressive-risk setup: limited narrative credit today, meaningful rerating potential if execution improves.

The neutral view says “wait for confirmation.” But in markets, confirmation usually costs money. By the time HBM competitiveness is fully confirmed, DRAM/NAND pricing is fully reflected, labor risk is resolved, and technical indicators cool down, the stock may be materially higher. The point of an aggressive BUY is to enter when the probability-weighted upside is attractive, not when every uncertainty has disappeared.

Macro also supports taking the risk. Asian semiconductor names are being revalued as the core of the AI supply chain. Reuters and other sources are highlighting Samsung, SK Hynix, and TSMC as central beneficiaries. If global capital continues rotating into Asian AI infrastructure exposure, Samsung is one of the most liquid and obvious vehicles. Foreign flows can keep pushing large-cap leaders further than traditional valuation models expect, especially when earnings revisions are moving up.

And unlike many AI-linked names, Samsung is not just a narrative stock. It has real earnings, real cash flow, real pricing leverage, and an enormous balance sheet. That matters as the AI trade enters a “show me the profits” phase. Samsung can show the profits through memory ASPs, margin expansion, and cash generation. That gives it better quality than many expensive AI-adjacent companies.
