But that is exactly the problem.

The bull argument assumes that strong fundamentals automatically justify buying after a vertical move. I disagree. In cyclical semiconductors, the most dangerous time to buy is often when the story feels most obvious, margins look unbeatable, customers are panic-buying capacity, and the stock appears “cheap” on peak earnings.

That is where SK hynix looks today.

# 2. The “Forward P/E of 4.56x” Argument Is Not as Bullish as It Sounds

The bull leans heavily on the forward P/E of **4.56x**. I understand why. On the surface, it looks extremely cheap.

But in memory semiconductors, low P/E ratios are often **not buy signals**. They are often **cycle-peak warning signs**.

Why? Because the “E” in the P/E may be inflated by temporary scarcity, unusually strong pricing, and peak margins.

SK hynix’s current profitability is extraordinary:

- Q4 2025 operating margin: about **58.4%**
- Q4 2025 net margin: about **46.4%**
- Reported TTM profit margin: **56.9%**
- ROE: **61.17%**
- ROA: **27.89%**

Those are remarkable numbers — but they are also exactly the kind of numbers investors should be careful extrapolating.

The bull says: “Even if we haircut margins, valuation still looks undemanding.”

But how much of a haircut are we talking about?

Memory margins do not gently normalize. They can collapse when supply catches demand, inventory builds, or pricing rolls over. If operating margins move from the current extreme levels toward more normalized semiconductor-cycle levels, forward earnings can fall very quickly.

That is why the market is giving SK hynix a low multiple. The market is not stupid. It is saying: **these earnings may not be durable.**

Also, let’s not ignore the PEG ratio: **2.38**. That is not screaming “undervalued.” It suggests the growth story is already being capitalized.

So no, I do not see 4.56x forward earnings as a clean bargain. I see it as a classic cyclical trap risk: **cheap on peak earnings, expensive if the cycle turns.**

# 3. The Bull Calls Big Tech Funding Offers a Structural Positive — I See a Potential Cycle-Top Signal

The Reuters report that Big Tech customers may support new production lines, equipment purchases, or dedicated supply is clearly bullish in the short term.

But the bull treats this as proof that SK hynix’s risk has permanently declined. I think that is too optimistic.

Customer-backed capacity can be a double-edged sword.

Yes, it improves near-term visibility. But it also encourages supply expansion. And memory cycles usually turn when everyone believes supply will stay tight forever.

The bull says HBM capacity is hard to add. True. But hard does not mean impossible. SK hynix, Samsung, Micron, and equipment suppliers are all economically incentivized to expand. If Big Tech helps fund capacity, that accelerates the process.

In other words, the same thing bulls celebrate — customers subsidizing capacity — may become the seed of the next oversupply cycle.

And dedicated production lines are not automatically good. They can also mean:

- Higher customer concentration
- Greater dependence on a few hyperscalers
- More contractual rigidity
- More pressure to deliver capacity on customer timelines
- Potentially capped economics if long-term pricing is locked in
- Higher fixed-cost burden if demand slows later

The bull frames customer financing as “lower-risk growth CapEx.” Maybe. But it can also be **CapEx discipline breaking down under euphoric demand conditions**.

That is how semiconductor cycles usually get dangerous.

# 4. HBM Is Strategic — But It Is Not Immune to Competition

The bull argument treats HBM almost like a protected monopoly. I would be careful with that.

Yes, SK hynix is extremely well positioned in HBM today. Yes, HBM is harder than commodity DRAM. Yes, qualification, yields, and packaging create barriers.

But barriers are not permanent.

Samsung and Micron are not standing still. Customers do not want to be dependent on one supplier for a mission-critical component. Hyperscalers and GPU vendors have every incentive to qualify multiple sources and reduce supplier pricing power over time.

That means SK hynix’s current bargaining power may be strongest **right now**, when supply is tight and competitors are catching up.

The bull says Big Tech’s scramble proves SK hynix is a bottleneck asset. I agree — today. But bottleneck assets attract capital, competition, and customer pressure. The more profitable the bottleneck becomes, the more aggressively the ecosystem tries to eliminate it.

That is the normal lifecycle of semiconductor scarcity.

# 5. Customer Cost Pressure Is Not Purely Bullish

The bull says Sony and Nintendo complaining about memory prices proves SK hynix has pricing power.

Again: yes, in the short term.

But this is where I think the bull case is too one-sided. When customers publicly cite memory cost pressure, that is not just confirmation of supplier power. It is also a warning that price elasticity is beginning to matter.

Nintendo reportedly expects around **100 billion yen** of cost pressure from components and tariffs. That is not trivial.

If memory prices keep rising, downstream companies have three choices:

1. Absorb margin pressure  
2. Raise consumer prices  
3. Cut orders, redesign products, or delay launches  

For AI hyperscalers, demand may be less price-sensitive today. But not all of SK hynix’s business is HBM for AI data centers. DRAM and NAND still touch PCs, smartphones, gaming devices, servers, storage, and consumer electronics.

The bull wants to separate AI demand from consumer demand. That is fair. But SK hynix’s earnings leverage is currently benefiting from broad DRAM/NAND pricing strength too. If non-AI demand weakens because pricing becomes punitive, the “broad-based recovery” thesis becomes less robust.
